THE DRAGON THRONE
Chapter XXVII · The Three Sons of Heaven · January–December 1440

Beijing, the sixteenth year of your reign.

The Great Ming is changing faster than its officials can comprehend.

Your treasury has become measurable. Your mines produce gold and iron. Your banks finance merchants. Your academies are beginning to teach mathematics and engineering alongside the classics.

The Crown Prince has shared a divine dream with you.

And now, on the morning after the year's first Grand Council, Heaven summons your second son.

The dream is different.

Where Jing saw fields, canals and institutions, Yan sees banners, horses and cities whose names have never appeared on a Ming military map.

The dragon has apparently chosen a different destiny for each brother.

One shall build the richest empire in history.

The other shall command its greatest armies.

I. The Dragon's Third Visitation
Second night of the first month, 1440 — Prince Yan's chambers

Prince Zhu Yan, now twenty-one, dreams that he stands on a battlefield beneath a blood-red sky.

He wears armour of blackened steel. Behind him stretches an army so vast that its banners disappear beyond the horizon.

Before him lies an immense river.

Beyond it rise the walls of a foreign city.

A voice shakes the earth.

"ZHU YAN."

The prince falls to one knee.

A golden dragon descends through the clouds.

"You have spent your life wondering why Heaven summoned your father and brother, but not you."

Yan raises his head.

"Great Dragon, I have served them faithfully."

"Have you never wished for the throne?"

Yan hesitates.

"Sometimes."

The dragon's eyes narrow.

"Then listen carefully."

The landscape changes.

Yan sees Jing seated upon the Dragon Throne, older and crowned.

He sees himself standing beside his brother in the armour of a Grand Marshal.

Then the dragon shows him an extraordinary vision.

Armies crossing the mountains beyond Yunnan.

Cavalry riding across the Central Asian steppes.

Siege engines advancing toward distant fortresses.

The banners of the Ming flying beside the great rivers of Mesopotamia.

The Tigris.

The Euphrates.

Cities with names unfamiliar to Yan: Baghdad, Mosul, Basra.

The dragon speaks.

"YOUR BROTHER SHALL BUILD THE WEALTH. YOU SHALL COMMAND THE POWER THAT DEFENDS IT."

Yan sees himself winning battles against armies from kingdoms he cannot identify.

He sees cavalry manoeuvres, fortified river crossings and vast logistical networks.

Then the vision turns darker.

A battlefield covered with broken spears.

Thousands of dead.

Yan himself, older and gravely wounded, lying beneath a torn imperial banner.

His brother kneels beside him.

The dragon says:

"Your glory may outlive even your brother's prosperity. Men may remember you as the greatest general who ever lived."

Yan whispers:

"Then I shall conquer the world."

The dragon roars.

"NOT FOR YOURSELF."

The prince recoils.

"Your mandate is not to rule. It is to lead."

The dragon lowers its head.

"If you seek your brother's throne, your victories shall become the ruin of your house. If you defend his reign, your name shall be spoken long after the names of kings are forgotten."

Yan looks again toward the distant armies.

"Will I truly reach Mesopotamia?"

"You have seen what might be. Not what must be."

"Will I die there?"

"Every general dies. Few choose what their lives shall mean."

The dragon's final words strike him harder than any sword.

"BE THE SWORD OF THE DRAGON THRONE, NEVER ITS RIVAL."

Yan awakens screaming.

The Chinese already possess centuries of woodblock printing experience and earlier traditions of movable type, including ceramic and wooden types. Your innovation is the systematic combination of reusable type, improved ink application, repeatable pressure and organised commercial printing.

Ink and paper

Engineers experiment with ink viscosity, paper dampness and printing pressure. Traditional woodblock printing remains preferable for some illustrated or repeatedly printed works.

The first technical problem
The prototype works.

But Chinese writing presents a practical challenge.

A European alphabetic printer can compose pages from a relatively small set of letters. A Chinese printer must manage hundreds or thousands of characters, with different frequencies of use.

The Academy therefore develops a standardised type inventory organised by character frequency and retrieval index.

Common characters receive large stocks.

Rare characters can be cast or carved as needed.

For long runs of unchanged texts, traditional woodblocks may still be cheaper. For frequently revised laws, examination materials, financial forms and technical manuals, reusable type and press production can offer substantial advantages.

You assemble the Ministers of Revenue, Works and War, the directors of the Imperial Investment House, representatives of merchant guilds and the Imperial Academy's senior engineers.

You write four words on a large board:

TEXTILES. AGRICULTURE. IRON. BANKING.

"These are not four separate industries," you announce. "They are one system."

You explain your vision.


Illustrative economic feedback loop: agricultural surpluses support workers; textiles create demand for machinery; metalworking supplies equipment; banks finance investment and trade.
The Crown Prince immediately understands.

"Father, if the farmers produce more food, more people can work in manufacturing. The factories purchase machinery, the ironworks supply it, and the banks finance the expansion."

"Precisely."

Yan asks:

"And the ironworks can eventually supply our arsenals?"

"Yes."

Xia Yuanji interrupts.

"Provided the banks do not lend recklessly, the factories actually sell their goods, and the state does not subsidise every enterprise indefinitely."

You point at him.

"That is why you are still Minister of Revenue."

II. The Textile Mills of Jiangnan
Xuanwu Lake and Jiangning Weaving in the Early Ming Dynasty - iNEWS
Second month — Suzhou

You travel south with Crown Prince Jing to inspect the silk and cotton industries.

Jiangnan already possesses sophisticated textile production, experienced artisans, commercial networks and access to waterways.

You do not need to invent weaving.

You need to increase the amount of useful work that can be performed with a given number of workers.

At a riverside workshop, you inspect a large waterwheel.

The master craftsman explains that its motion powers equipment used in preparing fibre.

You ask:

"Could it power several machines through shafts and belts?"

"Yes, Your Majesty, although maintaining steady motion is difficult."

"And could we use it to drive winding equipment?"

"With suitable gearing."

"Then build a workshop where one wheel drives several operations."

The craftsman bows.

The first imperial textile demonstrations
The Academy works with private textile merchants to develop three experimental sites.

The initial focus is on winding, reeling, fibre preparation and selected finishing operations, where water power is easier to apply.

Fully mechanised spinning and weaving remain much more difficult because they require precise, reliable movements and consistent thread quality.

The first trials experience frequent belt failures, irregular winding and broken threads.

One merchant complains:

"Your Majesty, the machine produces more cloth only when it is not being repaired."

You laugh.

"Then the engineers have discovered their next task."

By autumn, one workshop demonstrates a useful improvement in winding productivity, while the other two remain experimental.

No empire-wide textile productivity gain is claimed.

The private merchants fund most equipment and operations; the Academy's engineering support is included in its industrial research programme.

III. The Emperor Demands Coke-Fired Iron
Third month — Daye Iron District


The Daye engineers present samples of coal from the divine atlas's mapped deposits.

You ask them to explain the difference between coal and coke.

A metallurgist answers:

"Coal contains substances that smoke and interfere with the furnace. Heating suitable coal in a limited supply of air may produce a stronger carbon-rich fuel."

You nod.

"Then test it."

The engineers explain that Chinese metalworkers have long experimented with coal and coke-like fuels, but reliable large-scale coke smelting requires appropriate coal, controlled preparation and suitable furnace operation.

The dragon's maps identify coal seams and likely qualities. They do not automatically solve furnace chemistry.

The first coke trials
The Academy establishes a controlled programme at selected ironworks.

The objectives are to compare fuel consumption, furnace temperatures, metal quality and operating reliability.

The first trials are mixed.

Some fuel batches crumble.

Others contain undesirable impurities.

One experimental furnace produces useful iron, but its output is inconsistent.

The metallurgist warns:

"Your Majesty, we have demonstrated that this fuel may be useful. We have not yet demonstrated that it is cheaper or better than charcoal in every furnace."

You answer:

"Then continue testing. I want records, not flattery."

The Ministry orders comparative trials rather than compulsory conversion of all ironworks.

By year-end, coke preparation is a promising research programme, not a completed metallurgical revolution.

The difficulty of inventing steam centuries early
Your knowledge gives the engineers a conceptual destination.

It does not provide precision boring machines, dependable seals, suitable alloys, standardised valves or an established culture of pressure-vessel inspection.

The first prototypes leak.

The second generation performs somewhat better.

The third demonstrates controlled mechanical movement on a laboratory scale.

By December, the Academy has produced a functioning small experimental steam apparatus, but no useful industrial mine-pumping engine.

The director estimates that a reliable stationary pumping machine could take years of sustained development, even with your guidance.

You are disappointed.

Then Jing reminds you:

"Father, a year ago they had no organised steam research programme at all."

You smile.

"Then we have begun."

Fifth month — Northern Military District

Prince Yan is impatient.

He has commanded six thousand soldiers for more than a year. His formation is increasingly disciplined. Yet you have ordered him to concentrate on supply, sanitation, transport and military medicine.

You send Crown Prince Jing to accompany him on a second major logistics exercise.

The Thirty-Day Campaign Experiment
The War Ministry orders the six-thousand-man professional formation to undertake a simulated campaign without requisitioning food from local farmers.

Its objectives are to measure the effectiveness of planned supply routes, reserve depots, transport arrangements and field medical services.

Zhang Fu oversees the exercise.

By the second week, Yan discovers three problems.

First, the baggage animals consume a substantial proportion of the food transported for the army.

Second, several roads marked as passable are unsuitable for heavily loaded wagons after rain.

Third, the soldiers' sickness rate is influenced as much by camp hygiene and water quality as by combat training.

Jing's accountants calculate the cost of every day's delay.

Yan complains:

"Brother, your ledgers are more frightening than enemy cavalry."

Jing replies:

"Enemy cavalry might kill a thousand men. A failed supply system could kill all six thousand."

VI. The Crown Prince and the Bankers
Sixth month — Nanjing Commercial Exchange

Jing meets representatives of the Soldiers' Bank, private banking houses and merchant guilds.

You have instructed them to expand productive commercial lending without creating a banking crisis.

Jing proposes a system in which loans to established businesses are evaluated using audited accounts, collateral and demonstrated repayment capacity.

Transferable bills of exchange continue developing, with clearer rules for endorsement, settlement and dishonour.

The bankers are enthusiastic.

One merchant asks:

"Will the imperial government guarantee the debts of every factory?"

Jing answers:

"No."

The merchant looks disappointed.

"Then what is the benefit of the reform?"

"That reliable businesses can obtain credit without needing a personal favour from an official."

The Crown Prince also insists that banks must not treat the imperial connection as permission to lend without scrutiny.

You approve the policy.

The state will encourage industrial finance, not guarantee every industrial failure.

THE DRAGON THRONE
Chapter XXXI · The Army That Pays for Itself · January–December 1444

Beijing, the twentieth year of your reign.

For nearly two decades, you have pursued wealth through mining, trade, agriculture, banking and industry.

Now you turn your attention to the greatest expense of the Ming state: the military establishment.

Your six-thousand-man professional formation is becoming better trained and more disciplined. Prince Yan has matured into a capable commander. The Imperial Academy is developing better machinery, while your officials are learning to measure productivity rather than merely count expenditure.

But a question has begun to trouble you.

Why should a more professional army necessarily be a more expensive army?

You summon the Grand Council.

"Generals, ministers, my sons—I have a new objective. I want the best army in the world, but I also want the cheapest army capable of delivering that level of effectiveness."

Xia Yuanji looks up immediately.

"Your Majesty, this may be the finest decree you have ever issued."

Yan grins.

"Father, you have finally found a way to make the Minister of Revenue enthusiastic about soldiers."

You continue:

"We shall not reduce the army's effectiveness to save silver. We shall eliminate everything that consumes silver without producing military power."

Zhang Fu bows.

"Then, Your Majesty, we must begin by examining the army's accounts."

I. The Great Military Efficiency Commission
First month — Hall of Military Eminence

You establish the Imperial Commission for Military Efficiency, jointly chaired by Zhang Fu and Xia Yuanji.

Prince Yan represents operational commanders.

Crown Prince Jing supervises the financial methodology and independent audit.

For the first time, the War Ministry must answer not merely how many soldiers it maintains, but what military capability those soldiers actually provide.

You expressly forbid arbitrary cuts to soldiers' rations, wages or necessary equipment.

"Any official who saves money by starving soldiers will answer to me personally."

Zhang Fu nods.

"Then the commanders will understand that efficiency means readiness, not deprivation."

The central principle
Jing introduces a new administrative measure:

Cost per effective soldier.

The Commission distinguishes between soldiers appearing on a payroll and soldiers who are trained, equipped, medically fit and available for duty.

A unit with 10,000 names but only 6,000 deployable soldiers may be more expensive per effective soldier than a properly maintained professional force of 7,000.

This becomes the central principle of the reforms.

II. The Discovery That Shakes the War Ministry
Second month — Northern Military District

The Commission begins with a limited audit of 40 hereditary military units.

The results are uncomfortable.

Some soldiers are absent but remain on the rolls.

Others are serving as agricultural labourers or performing private work for their commanders.

Several units possess equipment recorded as serviceable that is actually damaged or missing.

Jing presents the findings.

"Father, the difficulty is not simply that the military costs too much. We cannot consistently establish what we are purchasing with the money."

You turn to Zhang Fu.

"How widespread is this?"

"We do not yet know, Your Majesty. These are forty units, not the entire army."

"Then do not extrapolate the figures across the empire."

Xia Yuanji nods approvingly.

The audited pilot findings
Within the forty-unit sample, the Commission identifies approximately 11% of recorded personnel positions requiring correction, investigation or reclassification.

This does not mean 11% of the entire Ming army is fictitious.

Some discrepancies arise from delayed transfers, deaths, illness, detached duty and incomplete records.

Others are genuine abuses.

You order officials to distinguish administrative errors from fraud before imposing penalties.

The first savings
The Commission corrects duplicate payments, removes verified ineligible entries and consolidates selected purchases.

By the end of the year, these reforms produce 42,000 taels in verified annualised net savings, after allowing for replacement administrative costs.

They become effective in the 1445 recurring budget.

No additional savings from the rest of the army are assumed.

Xia Yuanji announces:

"Your Majesty, we have found recurring savings without dismissing a single necessary soldier."

You smile.

"Then continue."

III. Prince Yan's New Army Becomes More Efficient
Third month — Imperial Training Grounds

Yan's six-thousand-man formation becomes the Commission's principal operational laboratory.

You give him a direct order.

"Reduce the cost of maintaining your formation without reducing its fighting capability."

Yan asks:

"May I reduce the number of accountants?"

Jing answers before you can speak.

"No."

The formation's reforms
Yan introduces standardised training schedules, equipment inspections and regular maintenance.

The quartermasters adopt common stock registers.

The engineers improve wagon loading and repair procedures.

Medical officers enforce camp sanitation rules.

The training programme is redesigned so soldiers spend less time waiting for equipment or supplies.

The results are modest but measurable.

Yan's argument with the generals
An older commander complains:

IV. The Emperor Rejects a Dangerous Proposal
Fourth month — Grand Council

A provincial official suggests saving money by reducing the number of cavalry horses.

Another proposes replacing some soldiers' grain allowances with lower-quality supplies.

You reject both proposals.

"The northern frontier requires cavalry. And I will not save silver by weakening the men who defend us."

Instead, you order the War Ministry to examine horse breeding, veterinary care, forage storage and the cost of replacing animals.

The Academy proposes improved harness fittings and wagon components.

Jing suggests comparing local procurement with transporting supplies over long distances.

Zhang Fu observes:

"Your Majesty, the cheapest grain at the place of purchase may become the most expensive grain by the time it reaches the army."

You reply:

"Then calculate the delivered cost, not merely the purchase price."

The Commission adopts this principle across its procurement trials.

V. The Reform That Could Save Millions
Fifth month — Ministry of Revenue

Xia Yuanji brings you a memorandum.

"Your Majesty, we have examined the structure of military expenditure. There is a much larger opportunity than the savings from the first forty units."

You lean forward.

"How much larger?"

"Potentially several hundred thousand taels annually. But only after we establish the true condition of the army."

He explains that the greatest long-term efficiencies may come from five structural changes.

First: reduce non-effective military expenditure. Correct hereditary rolls, eliminate unauthorised payments and ensure that military households perform their obligations or are properly reclassified.

Second: increase readiness without proportionally increasing headcount. A smaller effective force may be more valuable than a larger force that cannot be deployed.

Third: consolidate procurement. Establish regional contracts for uniforms, grain, tools and equipment, with competition and independent inspection.

Fourth: maintain equipment rather than constantly replace it. Extend the useful lives of wagons, harnesses, weapons and other military property.

Fifth: reduce the cost of campaigning. Better roads, depots, sanitation and supply planning can lower losses from spoilage, sickness and transport.

VI. The Steam Engine Meets the Gold Mine
Sixth month — Imperial Academy

You return to the Academy.

Its engineers have spent another year studying atmospheric pressure, condensation, metalworking and pumps.

The director greets you beside a large experimental apparatus.

"Your Majesty, we have made progress."

"Does it pump water?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

He hesitates.

"Not very much."

You laugh.

"Then we shall call it progress rather than victory."

The apparatus is a small, stationary demonstration. It produces intermittent pumping action, but leaks, friction and inconsistent operation make it less practical than existing water-powered pumps.

You ask whether it can be installed in a gold mine.

"Not safely or economically, Your Majesty."

"Then what is missing?"

"Better cylinders, tighter seals, reliable valves, stronger metalworking and much more testing."

The Academy's breakthrough
The most important result of 1444 is not the steam engine itself.

It is the development of better precision-measuring tools and improved boring techniques for cylindrical components.

The engineers also establish a formal inspection register for experimental boilers and pressure vessels.

The Academy director explains:

"Even if the steam machine fails, these tools will improve pumps, waterwheels, ironworks and other machinery."

You smile.

"Then the research has already begun to repay us."

The Academy's industrial programme receives 40,000 taels in extraordinary Treasury payments during 1444, including steam research and associated metalworking.

No commercially useful steam engine exists yet, and no steam-powered riverboat is constructed.

VII. The First Industrial Network
Seventh month — Jiangnan

Your industrial policy begins producing results.

The three experimental water-powered textile workshops are evaluated.

One has become commercially promising.

Another remains unreliable.

The third has been redesigned to concentrate on simpler mechanical operations.

The Imperial Academy concludes that the greatest immediate opportunities lie in improving established processes rather than attempting fully mechanised textile factories at once.

"Then perhaps one day the empire will experience an industrial revolution that does not require me to finance it personally."

X. The Inner Palace — Two Visitors from Distant Lands
Tenth month — Forbidden City

Lady Dilshad arrives with a small Central Asian delegation.

She is thirty-three, educated, confident and familiar with the commercial networks connecting China with Samarkand and the western Islamic world.

Lady Ratna arrives several weeks later, accompanied by relatives and merchants from the Malay world.

Empress Shen receives both women formally.

You meet them separately before inviting them to a family banquet.

Lady Dilshad
At your first private audience, Dilshad asks:

"Your Majesty, is it true that your banks allow merchants to transfer obligations between distant cities?"

"Yes."

"And that you are developing machines powered by steam?"

"We are attempting to."

She smiles.

"Then your empire is more interesting than the stories suggested."

You ask whether she would consider remaining at court.

She answers:

"I would first like to know whether an imperial consort may continue studying and corresponding with her family."

You assure her that reasonable personal correspondence and intellectual pursuits would be respected, subject to ordinary court security.

She agrees to extend her visit.

No marriage is concluded.

Lady Ratna
Ratna is particularly interested in maritime trade.

She asks whether a marriage would give her family preferential access to the Eastern Seas Trading Company.

You answer:

"No. They may invest on the same commercial terms as other qualified merchants."

She laughs.

"Then you are either a very principled emperor or a very difficult negotiator."

"Perhaps both."

She also agrees to remain for further introductions.

The Empress's assessment
After the banquet, Shen asks:

"Which one interests you more?"

You consider the question.

"Dilshad understands the western trade routes. Ratna understands the seas."

The Empress sighs.

"I asked which woman interests you, not which trade route."

You laugh.

"Both are intelligent. I would like to know them better."

"Then do so before discussing marriage."

You agree.

The imperial children
Princess Mingyue, now a toddler, has begun walking unsteadily through the Inner Palace.

Thiri spends much of her time with her daughter.

Princess Xinyi takes an active role in the educational and charitable work supervised by the Empress.

Jing continues his administrative responsibilities.

Yan visits the family whenever military duties permit.

At a private dinner, Yan lifts Mingyue into his arms.

"Little sister, one day I shall show you the cavalry."

Thiri replies:

"Perhaps first teach her not to fall over."

The family laughs.

The Empress later reminds you that dynastic stability requires time and attention, not merely more marriages and children.

You accept the criticism.

XI. The Emperor's Private Council — What Is an Army Worth?
Eleventh month — Hall of Military Eminence

The Commission presents its final recommendations.

Zhang Fu speaks first.

"Your Majesty, the professional army has become more reliable. But its greatest value may be that it has taught us how to measure the rest of the military establishment."

Xia Yuanji agrees.

"We should not convert every hereditary soldier into a salaried professional immediately. That would increase recurring expenditure faster than we can finance it."

Jing proposes a staged model.

The first tier: A professional core of well-trained, salaried troops maintained at high readiness.

The second tier: Existing hereditary and provincial formations, progressively audited and reorganised where necessary.

The third tier: Specialised engineers, artillery personnel, cavalry and logistics units, with higher training standards.

The fourth tier: Local defence and support arrangements appropriate to provincial circumstances, rather than maintaining expensive full-time formations everywhere.

Yan supports the proposal.

"But I want the professional core to expand eventually."

You answer:

"It will. When the Treasury can afford it and when you have demonstrated that the additional soldiers are necessary."

The 1445 military programme
You authorise continued audits, procurement improvements and training reforms within the existing establishment.

The Commission must prepare a costed plan for eventual expansion of the professional core to 12,000 soldiers.

No expansion beyond 6,000 is approved yet.

The generals must also identify opportunities to reduce unnecessary administrative expenditure without undermining military households or frontier defence.

Zhang Fu bows.

"Your Majesty, we shall build an army that is stronger because it is better organised, not merely because it is larger."

XIII. The Dragon's Fifth Prophecy
Winter solstice, 1444

The three of you—Emperor, Crown Prince and Prince Yan—meet privately in the Hall of Ancestral Records.

Jing brings the annual fiscal statement.

Yan brings the military reform report.

You bring the Academy's drawing of its experimental steam pump.

You place all three documents on the same table.

"Look at them."

Yan studies the papers.

"An army, a treasury and a machine."

"Exactly."

Jing understands first.

"Each depends on the others."

You nod.

"An efficient army protects commerce. Commerce produces wealth. Wealth finances education and engineering. Engineering makes industry more productive. And a richer industry can supply a stronger army."

Yan smiles.

"Then I suppose my brother and I shall both become indispensable."

"You already are."

That night, Yan tells Jing something he has never said aloud.

"Brother, when Father is gone, I will command your armies. But I will never seek your throne."

Jing looks at him.

"And I will never treat you as a threat merely because soldiers respect you."

The brothers embrace.

For the first time, the prophecy feels less like a warning and more like a promise.
